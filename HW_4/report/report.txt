CS380P Lab 4: Two-Phase Commit in Rust

Kyle Stanford


Introduction

This lab implements two-phase commit with a coordinator, a set of participants and a set of
clients. Each role is a separate process started from the same Rust binary, and they talk over
ipc-channel. The coordinator decides every transaction. Run mode takes -s, the chance that a
participant's operation succeeds, and -S, the chance that a vote message is actually delivered.
I ran each configuration three times and report the mean. All 51 runs passed the checker.


Hardware

All measurements were taken on a Codio VM with 8 cores. The VM is shared with other tenants, so
the numbers are noisy. Times are wall clock for the whole run, including process startup.


Section 1: Approach

Run mode starts the coordinator, which spawns each participant and client as a child process of
the same binary. Each child connects back through a one-shot IPC server whose name is passed with
--ipc_path, creates two channels, and sends the coordinator its ends, so every child has one
channel each way to the coordinator. This works because ipc-channel senders and receivers can
themselves be sent over a channel.

The coordinator handles one transaction at a time. When a client request comes in it sends a
propose to every participant and collects the votes, then logs the commit or abort, sends the
decision to all participants and sends the result to the client. Clients send one request and
wait for its result before sending the next.

On a propose, a participant performs the operation, which succeeds with probability s, logs its
vote and sends it. The send is dropped with probability 1 minus S, which is how message loss is
simulated. On a decision, it logs the decision.

The ipc-channel version pinned by the skeleton, 0.14, has no receive with a timeout. So the
coordinator starts one small thread per child that forwards its IPC receiver into a std mpsc
channel, and waits on those with recv_timeout. The vote timeout is 10 milliseconds. An abort vote,
or a missing vote after the timeout, aborts the transaction, since the coordinator does not know S.
A vote that arrives after its transaction was already decided is matched by txid and dropped.

The coordinator stops when all c*r requests are decided or on Ctrl-C, then sends an exit message
to every child. Each child prints its committed, aborted and unknown counts and exits, and the
coordinator waits on every child process before returning. A transaction caught mid-vote by
Ctrl-C is counted as unknown. Ctrl-C reaches every process, but only the coordinator knows whether
a transaction is in flight, so clients just stop sending and everyone else waits for the exit
message.


Section 2: Performance

Throughput did not scale with clients. With 10 participants and 200 requests per client, 1 client
ran at 457 transactions per second, 2 clients at 479, 4 at 462, 8 at 482, 16 at 420 and 20 at 366.

[GRAPH GOES HERE: clients.png]

The drop at 16 and 20 clients came from one slow run in each set, 9.8 seconds instead of 6.5 at 16
clients and 16.2 instead of 8.3 at 20. Those two runs also had 6 and 18 aborts with s and S both 1,
so votes missed the timeout while the VM stalled. Without them, 16 and 20 clients ran at about 490
and 483. So throughput stays around 460 to 490 regardless of client count. The coordinator is
sequential, so extra clients just queue up.

Throughput fell as participants were added. With 4 clients, 1 participant ran at 860 transactions
per second, 2 at 900, 4 at 817, 8 at 529 and 10 at 483.

[GRAPH GOES HERE: participants.png]

Each transaction costs a propose, a vote and a decision message per participant, all handled by
the single coordinator thread. One and two participants were about the same, so with few
participants the fixed cost per transaction dominates.

With 4 clients, 10 participants, S at 1 and 2400 transactions per value, the commit rate tracked
s^10: 0.578 at s=0.95 against an expected 0.599, 0.326 at s=0.90 against 0.349, and 0.101 at
s=0.80 against 0.107. Lower s was also slower, 1.90, 2.57 and 2.97 seconds against 1.65 at s=1,
which I did not expect since an abort vote lets the coordinator decide early. My guess is that the
remaining votes for an aborted transaction still arrive and have to be pulled and dropped during
the next one, which adds message traffic and context switching across about 15 processes and their
forwarder threads on 8 cores.

With s at 1, the commit rate tracked S^10 as well: 0.613 at S=0.95 against 0.599, 0.356 at S=0.90
against 0.349, and 0.098 at S=0.80 against 0.107. Here the cost is almost entirely the timeout.
Runs took 4.34, 6.43 and 8.12 seconds, and at S=0.80 about 721 of 800 transactions aborted, each
waiting out the full 10 milliseconds, which is about 7.2 seconds of the 8.1 second run.


Section 3: Difficulties and insights

The hardest decision was the timeout. A short one gives false aborts when the VM stalls, which is
where the 6 and 18 aborts above came from, while a long one makes every lost vote cost more. The
grader stops S runs at 120 seconds and can use up to 10000 requests per client, so 50 milliseconds
would have been too slow. I picked 10, which gave 4 false aborts in 20000 transactions in my tests.

Newer ipc-channel versions do have a timed receive, but newer crates do not build with Codio's
cargo 1.73, so I stayed on 0.14 and used the forwarder threads instead.

Rust's ownership rules made me decide things C++ would leave implicit. An IpcReceiver can be moved
to another thread but not shared, so each one has to belong to exactly one forwarder thread, and
the compiler enforces that. The same goes for the oplog and counters, which only the thread that
owns the coordinator or participant can touch. I did not hit a race or deadlock while testing,
which I credit to the compiler more than to me.
