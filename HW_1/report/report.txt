CS380P Lab 1: Prefix Scan and Barriers

Kyle Stanford


Introduction

This lab asked me to deliver a work-efficient parallel prefix scan using pthreads and then
measure it with the library barrier given to me, and then with a barrier I wrote myself. All the
runs I executed were on Codio which reported 8 cores in the environment. I ran each of the
configurations five times and kept the fastest run.

To implement the deliverable, I used the tree-based scan from the GPU Gems resource that was
recommended in the assignment links. We treat the array as a binary tree, where we walk up the
combining pairs until the last index holds the total for the entire array. We set the root to
zero, and our down-sweep walks back down, which pushes the partial sums into their place.
Generally, each sweep does about n operator calls, so total work is O(n).

That leaves an exclusive scan, so a third pass adds each input back into its own slot to make it
inclusive. I pad the array up to a power of two with zeros, since zero is the identity for the
operator and cannot change a real answer. Threads split the nodes active at each tree level,
with a barrier after every level because the next level reads what the previous one wrote. My
own barrier is a sense-reversing barrier on a pthread spinlock, where the last thread to arrive
flips a shared atomic flag and everyone else spins on it. The sense reversal is what makes it
safe to reuse, since consecutive rounds release on opposite values.

One detail drives most of my results. The two sweeps plus the inclusive pass do roughly three
times the operator calls the sequential loop does, so even though the algorithm is O(n), my best
possible speedup on 8 cores is about 8/3, or 2.67. I verified correctness by diffing parallel
output against the sequential path across five inputs and nine thread counts, with and without
my barrier. All 45 cases matched.


Step 1 Discussion

[GRAPH GOES HERE: speedup at -l 100000, pthread barrier]

Speedup here is sequential time divided by parallel time, so higher is better.

Two threads come out slower than sequential at about 0.66. Speedup then climbs through 4, 6 and
8 threads to roughly 2.1 to 2.3, and flattens around 2.2 to 2.5 after that.

The slowdown at two threads surprised me until I did the arithmetic. Since the parallel version
does three times the work, two threads should land about 1.5 times slower, and I measured 1.53,
1.50 and 1.51 on the three inputs. Getting the same number on three input sizes told me this was
the work constant and not noise.

The climb is ordinary scaling, except the curve tracks p/3 instead of p, so it rises on a much
shallower slope than the ideal line. The flattening is just the core count. With 8 cores, extra
threads only add participants that everyone waits on at every barrier. My best was 2.47 against
the ceiling of 2.67, so nearly all of the gap to ideal is the work constant rather than bad
synchronization. Bigger inputs do slightly better, 2.47 on 16k against 2.24 on 1k, because work
grows with n while barrier count only grows with log n.


Step 2 Discussion

[GRAPH GOES HERE: speedup at -l 10, pthread barrier]

At 10 loops instead of 100000, the parallel version never wins at any thread count on any input.
On 1k the sequential scan takes 23 microseconds while two threads take 804, and 32 threads take
7637. The curve slopes downward instead of flattening, so every thread I add makes it worse.

The useful work disappeared but the overhead did not. At 10 loops an operator call is a few
instructions, so scanning 1024 elements is only microseconds of real work, and thread creation
plus 21 barrier crossings completely swamp it.

[GRAPH GOES HERE: inflection point, sequential vs parallel as -l grows]

Sweeping the loop count from 1 to 5000 at 8 threads, the crossover sits near 2500 on 1k, near
300 on 8k, and near 150 on 16k.

Changing this number flips the winner because it changes the work without changing the overhead.
On 1k the parallel time barely moves between 1 loop and 100, staying around 2300 to 2600
microseconds, while sequential climbs from 5 to 297 and tracks the loop count almost exactly.
The parallel version is sitting on a fixed floor of roughly 2500 microseconds and pays it no
matter how cheap the operator is. Bigger inputs cross earlier because they reach the same total
work at a lower loop count.

More generally, what matters is the cost of one operator call compared to the cost of one
barrier crossing. The loop count is just a convenient way to make operators of different
expense, and a real scan might be combining large structs or doing floating point work instead.
Parallelizing pays off when the work a thread does between two barriers is worth more than the
barrier itself, so cheap operators lose and expensive ones win.


Step 3 Discussion

[GRAPH GOES HERE: speedup at -l 10, both barriers]

    Structurally my barrier isn't that different from the pthread one, since both count arrivals
and release everyone once the count hits the thread total. The difference is what a waiting
thread actually does, because the pthread barrier puts early arrivers to sleep in the kernel and
gives up their core, while mine just spins on a flag and holds onto its core the whole time.

    Mine performs better when the threads fit on the cores, since the wait is short and we skip
the kernel entirely, and I measured it about 3 times faster up to 8 threads. The pthread barrier
performs better once we go past 8 threads, where I saw mine run 15 - 19 times slower at 10
threads and up to 89 times slower at 32.

    The pathological case for mine is more threads than cores, because the threads that already
arrived end up spinning on the exact cores the late threads need in order to run and arrive. For
the pthread barrier it's the opposite, with a lot of short phases while threads still fit on
cores, since you pay for the sleep and wake every single time even though the wait was brief.

    Repeating the part 2 measurements with my own barrier, the trends match up to 8 threads and
then split apart. At 100000 loops the pthread version levels off near 2.3 while mine peaks at 8
threads and drops to 0.79 on 1k at 32, which is worse than not parallelizing at all. This occurs
because past the core count the condition is either true or false rather than gradual, so at 9
threads at least one barrier per round is waiting on a thread that isn't even scheduled.

    As for the overheads, the work constant caps me at 2.67 before anything else goes wrong.
After that it is the top levels of the tree only having 4, then 2, then 1 active nodes so most
threads sit idle but still have to reach the barrier, plus the 31 barrier crossings and the
thread creation and teardown.


Insights

The main thing I took away is that work-efficient is a statement about big-O, and the constant
still decides what you actually measure. My scan is O(n) as required and also does three times
the work of the sequential loop, which is the difference between a ceiling of 8 and a ceiling of
2.67 on this machine. That single factor explains more of my graphs than anything about threads
or barriers.

The second is that spinning versus blocking is not a ranking, it depends on whether threads fit
on cores. My barrier is three times better than the library's on one side of that line and
nearly ninety times worse on the other, and the switch happens over two thread counts.
